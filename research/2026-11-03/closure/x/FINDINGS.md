# What the X exports add to the Pepsodent diagnosis

**Final X integration, 3 November 2026.** This analysis concerns public posts selected through user-run searches, not recruited consumers. It strengthens mechanisms to investigate; it does not measure the causes or prevalence of the case's Premium-to-Basic movement.

## Collection and information value

Four exports contain **16,165 occurrences and 12,990 distinct post IDs**, with **3,175 repeated-ID occurrences**. They contain 5,263 reply posts and no source-flagged retweets. One consumer story also appears in the earlier six-post known-URL collection. Its now-available full text replaces no original bytes: both sources remain recorded. The other five earlier posts are brand-owned and add IDs, so combined X coverage is **12,995 distinct platform IDs**.

The fourth run added 4,112 new IDs but repeated 2,528 already captured IDs. Broader collection therefore increased coverage with diminishing information per exported row. All new-export timestamps fall in January 2022–June 2026; geography and target age remain unverified.

Volume is highly concentrated. There are **6,814 source-account pseudonyms**, not verified people. One account contributes **1,401 posts** (10.8% of the new-export ID corpus), all labelled English by the source; inspected examples are repeated dollar-denominated deal lists. The next two accounts contribute 347 and 336 posts, including repeated import sales copy and promotional shopping links. This is a reason to inspect account/post concentration before interpreting common terms, not proof that every post by these accounts is a bot or paid ad.

There are **114 normalized exact-text repetition groups containing 461 IDs** and **375 possible template groups containing 2,152 IDs**. These sets overlap and must not be added. The largest template group has 219 posts about Colgate deals mixed with soap and phone chargers. Removing URLs, mentions, and numbers can merge distinct offers; these are signals for audit, not automatic deletion rules.

The rules flag 11,758 broad oralcare candidates, 212 accessory candidates, 559 ambiguous ODOL matches, 177 ambiguous brand matches, 249 unresolved others, 30 likely figurative mentions, and five obvious transport matches. **11,758 is not the number of relevant consumer accounts.** Close reading shows that toothpaste keywords readily retrieve media, gardening hacks, helmet cleaning, fiction, celebrity commentary, and mint-food comparisons.

Commerce markers are also substantial: 1,719 records contain detected retail destinations or commercial terms and 1,692 carry listing cues; the groups overlap. Shopping links can coexist with real use claims. We retain both interpretations rather than either declaring all endorsements independent or discarding every linked experience.

## First-person grammar is especially unsafe here

Close reading covered **109 records**: 40 uniformly sampled IDs, 60 sampled within purposive theme screens, and nine supplementary counterexamples/context checks. The source text, annotations, and selection method are in `evidence.csv`, `review_selection.json`, and `CODEBOOK.md`.

Six of the fixed 100 use repeated advertising-style ice-eating wording consistent with a familiar Sensodyne commercial narrative. The exact official advertisement source has not been independently matched in this analysis. One substitutes Pepsodent into the same narrative. They can match `saya`, `ibu`, `ngilu`, `dokter`, and brand terms without establishing independent patient outcomes, household-payer reports, or a completed brand switch. The revised automatic jingle screen flags 41 candidates across the full corpus, without claiming exhaustiveness.

Thus a rule such as “first-person + sensitivity + mother” can manufacture plausible-looking evidence. The primary research should ask for actual personal purchase histories and chronology, not simply collect agreement with a familiar commercial story.

## Mechanisms worth taking into interviews

### 1. The payer can block a desired brand, but the evidence is situational

Post **1927222520979058832** recalls wanting Closeup because of television advertising while the mother continued buying cheaper Ritadent. The writer now interprets the adult-only explanation as a budget rationale. This directly illustrates a gap between the desired product and the payer's decision. It is a retrospective childhood account, not evidence that most present-day 18–30-year-olds face the same constraint.

Post **1979392212954943852** describes a shared household toothpaste remaining from Unilever because parents are loyal and hard to persuade. That supports household choice authority, but neither exact Pepsodent SKU nor target age is established. Post **1837850186682716603** supplies a useful rival situation: the adult child buys household bathroom/kitchen essentials including toothpaste. “Young user” must not be equated with “parent pays.”

**Primary probe:** For the last tube, who wanted which product, who actually selected it, who paid, and which other people used it? Would a separate tube change both benefit fit and household spending?

### 2. Independent purchasing can enable uptrading

Post **2015590249167274297** reports moving after starting work from censored Pepsodent to a more expensive toothpaste and experiencing fewer mouth ulcers. This opposes an automatic story that independence forces downgrading. Employment can expand discretion or make a personally important benefit easier to fund. The post does not establish a clinical cause, exact SKU, verified age, or a specific premium tier.

**Primary probe:** What changed in available money, purchasing authority, shopping channels, and needs when the person began buying their own toothpaste? Allow upgrading, downgrading, and no change.

### 3. Weak whitening results do not always cause abandonment

Post **1600113707526524928** says there was no visible difference after months, but the person continued because the paste felt pleasant and did not provoke mouth ulcers. This matters because functional and experiential value can diverge: the stated specialist benefit can disappoint while comfort sustains use. Conversely, post **1737902732164673891** reports sensitivity after brushing with Pepsodent and a possible loss of fit. These are subjective experiences with unidentified formulation/tier and possible other causes.

**Primary probe:** What would the person miss if they stopped? Ask separately about noticed colour/sensitivity effects, comfort, taste, freshness, and familiarity. Avoid assuming the advertised benefit is the only relevant outcome.

### 4. Replacement can interrupt use even when the prior product was liked

Post **1540355497089728512** reports liking whitening and taste, changing toothpaste after running out, noticing colour return, then returning to the earlier paste. The reason for the intervening replacement is not stated; it cannot be assigned to stock, price, or household control. It nevertheless shows why “what was chosen when the tube ran out?” is more informative than current satisfaction alone.

Post **1637420629669601280** contrasts childhood monthly supermarket stock-up with present walkable minimarkets. Convenience can reduce the perceived need to stock up; it does not prove that any particular premium variant is available there. A Malay-language post **1734190351676756381** describes moving from Sensodyne/Oral-B to Systema because of price and rural-supermarket access. Its country is unverified, so it is a comparator mechanism, not Indonesian shelf evidence.

**Primary probe:** Reconstruct the actual replacement trip and available alternatives before showing any proposed concept. A liked product and a different next purchase can coexist.

### 5. Discounts can change inventory without demonstrating durable conversion

Post **1741625382271697407** claims buying four or five tubes when deeply discounted. Post **1787355139172671734** gives a free full-size Closeup tube to the mother, who is happy to use what is available instead of buying. These are stock-up/substitution stories. They do not measure incremental consumption, regular-price repeat, or paid retention after sampling.

**Primary probe:** How much toothpaste was already at home, how long did it last, what was purchased next, and at what paid price? A promotion can move purchase timing without permanently changing the preferred tier.

### 6. Paid price may be unknown to the user

Post **2068274183105286615** reports receiving Euthymol from an aunt and not knowing the actual price, later seeing an expensive asking price online. Gifted acquisition differs from demonstrated willingness to fund replenishment. Post **1842884914494296189** describes two small expensive tubes and postpones recommendation until trying them; pack size and benefit uncertainty both matter. Its ml reference must not be silently converted to grams.

**Primary probe:** Distinguish received, chosen, purchased, and paid-for products. Ask for pack size and actual transaction price, with uncertainty recorded if neither is known.

## What the legacy-overlapping consumer story really says

Post **2068234900491129031** is now available in full. It reports changing from imported Pasta Del Capitano to unspecified Pepsodent within a broader household-budget adjustment. This is a category downtrade narrative, **not a confirmed Pepsodent Premium-to-Basic switch**. Counting the earlier excerpt and the new full post twice would overstate independent corroboration. The source makes the affordability mechanism plausible while leaving the competition's exact transition unresolved.

## Selection and interpretation limits

Only 572 of 5,263 reply records have their conversation-root ID present, and no direct-parent field was available. Advice, irony, and missing referents are often unresolved. The actor language labels include 10,057 `in` records and 2,405 `en` records; an Indonesian-looking accessory listing was labelled `sl` in the reviewed sample. Language labels are not country verification.

No post here has independently verified target age, payer identity, actual SKU classification, paid price, or confirmed target switching history. A self-report is evidence of what was said, not proof that the behaviour or causal effect occurred. Clinical claims about detergent, SLS, whitening, mouth ulcers, or traditional practices remain unverified; they are not recommendations.

This corpus is sufficient to improve the questions and expose rival explanations. It is not sufficient to rank the causes of the seven-point case mix change, measure price elasticity, infer market sentiment from keyword counts, or choose a fixed intervention. The next useful data are contrasting actual purchase histories from confirmed eligible participants.
